Why We Do This

I started reading labels instead of trusting them.

This started as a parent's habit before it became a rubric. Here's the version without the spreadsheets, the age bands, or the citations.

The aisle

For a while, the front of the pack was enough.

Feeding kids well felt, for a long time, like it came down to reading the front of a box. "No added sugar." "Rich in fibre." "Trusted by nutritionists." I believed the front the way most parents do — because turning a pack over and reading nine lines of ingredient text in six-point type isn't something anyone has the time or patience for, standing in an aisle with a child pulling at your sleeve.

The flip

Somewhere along the way, I started turning packs over anyway.

Not out of suspicion at first — more out of habit, the way you check a price tag twice. And the gap between what the front claimed and what the ingredient panel actually said kept showing up in places I didn't expect. A "wholesome" muesli with sugar sitting a few ingredients down the list. A ketchup where tomato wasn't even the first thing named. A seasoning sachet whose printed "serving size" was so small that the sodium math only looked reasonable if nobody actually used a full sachet in a meal.

"The label wasn't lying. It just wasn't written for someone standing in an aisle with fifteen seconds and a tired kid."
The pattern

None of it was hidden. It just wasn't being read.

Everything I kept finding was printed, in compliance with the law, in the correct font size. This wasn't a story about brands breaking rules — it was a story about how much a food label can get away with saying, technically, truthfully, while still leaving a parent with the wrong picture of what's actually going into their child. The rules were being followed. The point was still being missed.

The decision

I didn't want another opinion about "good" and "bad" snacks.

There's enough of that already — inconsistent from one Instagram post to the next, and rarely benchmarked against anything more solid than a personal preference. What I wanted was the same numbers, read the same way, every time, checked against something real — ICMR-NIN's dietary guidelines, FSSAI's own regulations — instead of a brand's marketing copy. And I wanted our own products held to that same bar, not just everyone else's on the shelf.

What this became

Poshan Score is that habit, written down.

It's the same thing I was already doing at the shelf, turned into a rubric instead of kept in my head — six to ten numbers, the age bands they actually apply to, and the citations behind each one. It won't tell you what to feed your kids. That's still your call, and it should stay that way. It'll just tell you, plainly, what's actually in the pack before you decide.
